Definition & Classification of Elderly Age Ranges

Understanding chronological, biological, and functional gerontology boundaries based on World Health Organization (WHO) standards.

Comprehensive Elderly Definition

  • Biological Dimension: Gradual decline in functional organ reserve capacity (senescence).
  • Psychological Dimension: Adjustment to social role transitions, retirement, and ego integrity.
  • Sociocultural Dimension: The elderly's role as guardians of local wisdom or vulnerable groups.

Official WHO Classification

  • Middle Age: Ages 45 – 59 years.
  • Elderly: Ages 60 – 74 years (active young-old).
  • Old: Ages 75 – 90 years.
  • Very Old: Above 90 years (oldest-old).

Functional vs. Chronological Limits

  • Chronological Age: Calculated based on birth calendar.
  • Functional Age: Ability to perform daily activities (ADL & IADL).
  • Fitness Indicators: Gait speed and grip strength capacity.

Global Healthy Ageing (WHO) & ICOPE Framework

Decade of healthy ageing strategies, integrated care approaches, and intrinsic capacity screening for older adults

UN Decade of Healthy Ageing Program

The world's population is aging rapidly, with those aged 60 and over projected to surge from 1.1 billion to 1.4 billion. The main challenge is closing the gap between living longer and living healthier.

Vision & Slogan:

Creating a world where everyone can live long and healthy lives with the spirit of "adding life to years and years to life".

Four Strategic Action Areas

  • Combating Ageism: Changing how we think, feel, and act towards age and aging in society.
  • Age-Friendly Environments: Designing physical and social spaces to keep elderly participation active.
  • Integrated Care: Aligning primary services to be holistically centered around the individual.
  • Long-Term Care: Ensuring access to adequate long-term care support.

ICOPE Policy & Framework (Integrated Care for Older People)

WHO's innovative approach to redirect health services to be person-centered and coordinated at the primary care level, optimizing intrinsic and functional capacity in older adults.

Six Intrinsic Capacity Domains & Screening Tests:

Domain Assessed Aspect Quick Screening Test
Cognition Memory and time/place orientation Impairment noted if incorrect answers are given to either time/place orientation questions or failure to recall three words.
Mobility (Locomotion) Lower limb strength Chair rise test 5 times without arm support; indication of decline if it takes more than 14 seconds.
Vitality (Nutrition) Nutritional status & appetite Unintentional weight loss > 3 kg in the last 3 months, or experiencing significant loss of appetite.
Vision Functional vision impairment Evaluation based on vision problems reported directly by the patient.
Hearing Sensory hearing impairment Whisper test: failure to repeat two-syllable whispered words indicates hearing decline.
Psychological Depressive / affective symptoms Two questions regarding the past 2 weeks: feelings of sadness/hopelessness and loss of interest/pleasure in activities.

Indonesian Institutions & Elderly Welfare Laws

Roles of technical ministries, special legal foundations, national strategies, and cross-sector linkages

Role of the Indonesian Ministry of Health

The Ministry of Health is responsible for formulating, establishing, and executing policies in primary and community health, disease control, advanced healthcare, pharmacy, medical devices, and health human resources.

In elderly care, the Ministry focuses on promotive, preventive, curative, and rehabilitative efforts to address rising chronic disease risks and functional decline. It also provides technical guidance and supervision to local healthcare facilities.

Role of the Indonesian Ministry of Social Affairs

Based on Presidential Regulation No. 110 of 2021, the Ministry of Social Affairs assists the President in governmental affairs within the social sector.

The Ministry provides social protection and services for vulnerable, neglected, or economically constrained seniors through social rehabilitation, social protection, empowerment, and social assistance programs, positioning seniors as dignified subjects.

Main Legal Foundations: Law No. 13/1998 & Law No. 17/2023

Law No. 13 of 1998 on Elderly Welfare

Serves as the special legal basis distinguishing between potential elderly (capable of producing goods/services) and non-potential elderly (dependent on others), allowing tailored services.

Law No. 17 of 2023 on Health

Regulates public rights including seniors to receive comprehensive health services (promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative) and affirms shared responsibilities between central and regional governments.

National Strategy on Aging & Sector Integration

Presidential Regulation No. 88 of 2021 on National Strategy on Aging: Regulates cross-sector coordination to build independent, prosperous, and dignified seniors through enhanced social protection, age-friendly environments, and institutional strengthening.

Institutional Linkages: Elderly issues are multidimensional. The Ministry of Health handles medical aspects, while the Ministry of Social Affairs manages social and empowerment aspects. Cross-sector integration ensures holistic protection for seniors.

Healthy Ageing & Long-Term Care Programs in Indonesia

National primary healthcare initiatives, community empowerment, and long-term care (LTC) systems

Introduction to Healthy Ageing Programs in Indonesia

Healthy Ageing programs in Indonesia represent a continuum of efforts to maintain health, functional capacity, independence, and quality of life for older adults. Implementation spans early detection, health services, family empowerment, elderly education, up to long-term care.

A foundational framework is Primary Health Services Integration (ILP). Through ILP, primary healthcare is brought closer to communities and delivered across the life-course.

Primary Health Services Integration (ILP)

A healthcare service approach integrating life-course care, including elderly services via Puskesmas, auxiliary health posts (Pustu), integrated health posts (Posyandu), cadres, and home visits.

Emphasizes promotive, preventive, screening, treatment, rehabilitation, and follow-up care so conditions are detected early.

Simple Elderly Health Screening (SKILAS)

Simple screening to detect health issues and functional declines early before worsening.

Covers cognitive function, mobility, nutritional status, vision, hearing, and mental conditions for clinical referral.

Age-Friendly Community Health Center (Puskesmas Santun Lansia)

Puskesmas service approach addressing specific elderly needs and characteristics to sustain independence.

Encompasses promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative, counseling, education, and home visits upholding friendly, accessible principles.

Elderly Family Development (Bina Keluarga Lansia / BKL)

Family empowerment program aiming to enhance family capacity in caring for, accompanying, and meeting elderly needs.

Fosters the Resilient Elderly concept (seven dimensions: spiritual, physical, emotional, intellectual, social, vocational, and environmental).

Elderly School (Sekolah Lansia)

Non-formal education activity aimed at seniors and pre-seniors to boost knowledge, skills, health, independence, and social activity.

Curriculum covers physical-mental health, healthy lifestyles, disease prevention, physical activity, nutrition, social ties, and productivity supporting the SMART Elderly concept (Healthy, Independent, Active, Productive, and Dignified).

Long-Term Care (LTC) Implementation Schemes in Indonesia

Family-Based Care

Provided at home by family members as primary caregivers (assisting with ADLs, medication, nutrition). Offers comfort in a familiar environment but requires education and respite support to prevent caregiver burnout.

Elderly Posyandu / Community Care

Community-based healthcare integrated with ILP, bringing risk factor screening, health monitoring, and social interaction closer to maintain mental fitness.

Home Care Practice

Direct home services by health professionals or trained caregivers for nutrition monitoring, wound care, therapy, and medication compliance for high mobility-limited seniors.

Social Institutions / Nursing Homes

Institution-based LTC providing accommodation and intensive services for neglected seniors or those lacking adequate family support, including mental, spiritual, and comprehensive healthcare.

Japan's Institutions & Healthy Ageing Programs

Multisectoral approaches, legal frameworks, and strategic ministerial innovations for a healthy longevity society

Legal Framework & Macro Coordination (Cabinet Office)

Healthy ageing implementation in Japan is backed by multisectoral legal and policy frameworks. The cornerstone is the Basic Law on Measures for the Aging Society (Kōrei Shakai Taisaku Kihon-hō) enacted by the Japanese Parliament (Kokkai) in 1995. Under this framework, the Cabinet Office (Naikaku-fu) plays a central role in coordinating cross-sector policies to guarantee integrated elderly welfare.

Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW / Kōsei Rōdō-shō)

The primary agency responsible for healthcare, social welfare, long-term care, care prevention, social security systems, and promoting elderly independence.

Flagship Policy: 健康日本21（第三次) / Kenkō Nihon 21 – 3rd Term

Enacted by MHLW on May 31, 2023 for the 2024–2035 period, aiming to extend healthy life expectancy (kenkō jumyō) and reduce health disparities through three implementation pillars:

Dietary Improvement The *Shokuseikatsu Kaizen Fukyū Undō* movement focusing on adding 1 serving of vegetables and fruit daily by 2026.
Tokutei Kenshin Screening Risk factor check for ages 40–74. At-risk participants receive lifestyle guidance (*Tokutei Hoken Shidō*) integrated with health insurance.
2023 Sleep Guidelines Recommendations for seniors to avoid excessive bed rest, maintain daytime activity, and improve sleep habits.

Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT)

Supports safe physical environments, transportation, housing, and age-friendly public facilities.

Key Program (*Baria Furī Kihon Kōsō*): Integrated district planning (stations, sidewalks, public facilities) involving direct participation of seniors and disabled individuals (e.g., in Miyajima-guchi, Hatsukaichi City).

Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT)

Supports mental, social health, and lifelong learning opportunities for active seniors.

Three Main Slogans:
1. Shōgai Gakushū (Lifelong Learning)
2. Shōgai Supōtsu (Lifelong Sports)
3. Shakai Kyōiku (Social Education)

Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI)

Promotes technology, robotic innovations, and assistive devices for elderly independence.

Innovative Technology Examples:
• RT-3: Robotic intelligent walking aid with electric motors and automatic brakes.
• HAL for Care: Bio-electric skin signal reading robot for muscle rehabilitation.
• Navigation Toilet: Predictive micturition timing sensor.

Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC)

Focuses on communications and digital inclusion so seniors do not lag behind in information access.

Key Program: Dejitaru Katsuyō Shien Suishin Jigyō. Smartphone training and consultation for seniors to access online services, running across 6,000 locations since 2021.

Long-Term Care Insurance System (Kaigo Hoken) in Japan

Social security long-term care insurance system, beneficiary classifications, care levels, and joint financing

Introduction to Kaigo Hoken (介護保険)

Japan has a high proportion of older adults, leading to continuous growth in long-term care (LTC) needs. Japan implemented Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) or Kaigo Hoken in 2000 as a social security system to provide care access when physical or cognitive abilities decline and assistance with daily activities is required.

Kaigo Hoken reduces elderly reliance on families by sharing responsibilities among individuals, governments, municipalities, and society. Municipalities act as insurers managing enrollment, premiums, care needs certification, and provider payments, while the central government and prefectures support financing and system regulation.

Kaigo Hoken Beneficiary Categories

Participants are divided into two main categories based on age groups:

Category Provisions & Target
Category 1 Residents aged 65 and older. Benefits are granted following assessment and certification of support or care needs.
Category 2 Residents aged 40–64 years. Benefits are granted if care needs relate to specific aging-associated diseases.

Beneficiaries undergo care-needs certification involving physical and mental condition evaluations considering physician opinions. Provided services include home-visit care, home-visit nursing, day care, rehabilitation, short-stay, and facility care services.

Care Levels (Yōshien & Yōkaigo)

Care levels match services to dependency degrees. Support Levels (Yōshien) apply to individuals still relatively capable of daily activities but needing preventive support, while Care Levels (Yōkaigo) apply to individuals requiring assistance due to limitations in daily living activities.

Level Care Need Overview & Monthly Benefit Limits
Support 1 & 2 Light support and preventive services to prevent further functional decline.
Care Level 1 Assistance with partial activities, maintaining some independence. (Benefit limit approx. ¥167,650)
Care Level 2 More frequent assistance in daily activities. (Benefit limit approx. ¥197,050)
Care Level 3 Greater limitations requiring intensive care. (Benefit limit approx. ¥270,480)
Care Level 4 High dependency on others' assistance. (Benefit limit approx. ¥309,380)
Care Level 5 Highest care needs and intensive assistance across multiple activities. (Benefit limit approx. ¥362,170)

*Note: The figures above represent maximum service benefit limits, not direct cash payouts to users.

Kaigo Hoken Financing Structure

Kaigo Hoken utilizes joint financing from insurance premiums and public funds, with a general split of ~50% from premiums and ~50% from central and regional public funds.

Premium Sources (50%)

  • Category 1 Participants (≥65 yrs): Contributes ~23% (divided into 13 tiers based on economic capacity).
  • Category 2 Participants (40–64 yrs): Contributes ~27% (deducted through workers' health insurance).

Public / Government Funds (50%)

  • Central / National Government: ~25%
  • Prefectural Governments: ~12.5%
  • Municipal Governments: ~12.5%

In addition to premiums and public funds, service users pay a copayment generally set at 10% of service costs (while high-income individuals may pay 20% or 30%). This mechanism ensures equitable and sustainable social protection.

Japan's Technology in Long-Term Care and Healthy Ageing

Strategic roles of care robotics, smart sensors, and telehealth in supporting elderly functional independence and care sustainability

Technological Perspective in Long-Term Care & Healthy Ageing

Japan is among the nations facing surging needs for long-term care (LTC) due to a high elderly population. This condition drives the Japanese government to develop diverse technologies to assist care services while sustaining elderly independence.

Within the Healthy Ageing perspective, technology is utilized not only when seniors experience dependency, but also to maintain functional capacity, prevent health risks, and enable seniors to live independently as long as possible. WHO defines Healthy Ageing as the process of developing and maintaining functional ability enabling well-being in older age, emphasizing that technology helps maintain or enhance functional capacity to postpone intensive LTC needs.

Care Robotics in Long-Term Care

Use of robots or tech-based devices to assist elderly care activities. The Japanese government via MHLW and METI expanded care tech priority fields into 9 fields and 16 technology types (covering transfer, mobility, elimination, monitoring, bathing, caregiver support, functional training, nutrition, and dementia support).

Main Function: Alleviating heavy physical burdens on caregivers (e.g., transferring seniors) and assisting seniors directly via mobility devices. Aims to reduce physical strain while boosting safety and independence.

Smart Sensors and Monitoring Systems

Sensor technology to monitor elderly conditions and activities in real-time, in care facilities and homes alike.

Main Function: Detecting movements, fall risks, or physical condition changes early so caregivers receive prompt alerts. Helps seniors live safely at home while delaying formal or informal LTC needs. Implementation must uphold data privacy and security.

Telehealth and Healthcare Access

Remote healthcare services (*online medical care*) enabling patients to consult doctors digitally without always visiting clinics.

Main Function: Supporting *ageing in place* safely for seniors with mobility limitations or chronic conditions, combined with face-to-face visits per clinical needs.

Comparative Analysis of Technology's Role in Healthy Ageing

Summary of functions and direct linkages of Japanese technology in supporting LTC and Healthy Ageing systems:

Technology Role in Long-Term Care (LTC) Linkage to Healthy Ageing
Care robotics Assisting mobility, transfer, and care activities Sustaining independence and physical capability
Smart sensors Activity monitoring and risk detection Enhancing safety and enabling early detection
Telehealth Simplifying consultation and health monitoring Supporting service access and *ageing in place*

Japan's approach proves that the most effective technology is human-centered, where innovation reinforces caregiver roles and preserves social and interpersonal interactions rather than replacing them.

LTC Technology Adoption Feasibility & Constraint Strategies

Click each section below to explore adoption prospects, challenges, and ILP-based strategies

1. Comparative Prospects & Feasibility

Japan successfully utilizes care technology (robotics, sensors, ICT) to overcome labor shortages and sustain elderly independence. Indonesia holds strong potential for adoption through Primary Health Services Integration (ILP) and the SATUSEHAT digital ecosystem.

Rather than replicating Japan's entire complex model, Indonesia should selectively adopt cost-effective solutions: telehealth, vital sign tracking, basic safety sensors, and digital health records.

2. Main Challenges & Obstacles

First Constraint: Digital Literacy

Digital literacy remains a barrier among seniors, health cadres, and families. Seniors may struggle with using apps, authentication, reading small displays, or understanding digital instructions. Systematic reviews on telehealth find technical literacy to be among the most frequently reported barriers, while assistive tech reviews highlight usability and lack of knowledge as major hurdles.

💡 Solution: Implementing a 'digital-assisted care' model, where technology does not require seniors to be fully independent users. Posyandu cadres, families, or Puskesmas personnel act as digital assistants to measure parameters, input results into the system, and identify seniors needing follow-up care, preserving face-to-face interaction while leveraging digital records.

Second Constraint: Procurement Costs, Maintenance, and Connectivity

Procurement, device maintenance, and connectivity costs pose significant challenges. Japan's experience shows advanced technology requires major investment and upkeep, whereas Indonesia still faces regional disparities in digital readiness influenced by internet access, HR capacity, and budgets.

💡 Solution: Adoption should not begin with expensive robotics, but with high cost-benefit ratio technologies, such as basic monitoring devices, shared tablets at Posyandu, facility internet connections, and integrated recording systems.

Third Constraint: Network Disparities and System Interoperability

LTC digitization is less effective if data from Posyandu, Puskesmas, hospitals, and social services cannot be exchanged. SATUSEHAT was developed as an exchange ecosystem connecting various systems and health facilities, though regional implementation still faces infrastructure and resource variations.

💡 Solution: Applying interoperability principles and avoiding the creation of excessive standalone applications.

3. Flexible Solutions & ILP-Based Strategies

Flexible implementation strategies can be structured in four phased steps:

  • Stage One: Strengthening infrastructure and digital literacy at Puskesmas.
  • Stage Two: Developing Posyandu ILP as a technology-assisted elderly monitoring node.
  • Stage Three: Connecting monitoring results with Puskesmas for follow-up and clinical referrals.
  • Stage Four: Expanding telemonitoring, home sensors, or rehabilitation technologies based on heightened readiness.

✨ Conclusion: Adopting Japan's LTC technology in Indonesia is best achieved through phased adaptation rather than direct technology transfer. Puskesmas serve as clinical and data coordination hubs, while Posyandu ILP acts as a community network for screening, monitoring, education, and early detection. This approach preserves technology benefits—efficiency, data continuity, and remote monitoring—without sacrificing human-centered care at the core of elderly services.

Transcultural Nursing Reflection

Reflecting on transcultural nursing, family roles, formal support systems, and future global nursing competencies

Reflection on Transcultural Nursing

Elderly care is closely intertwined with culture, family values such as filial piety, and health policy systems. While families serve as the primary pillar of physical and emotional support, the complexity of long-term care (LTC) needs among older adults cannot be solely burdened onto families.

From a Transcultural Nursing perspective, nurses must be able to respect cultural values without neglecting the reality of family capacities. This highlights the importance of formal systems (such as service integration models and insurance schemes like those in Japan) which exist not to replace the family's role, but to complement and strengthen the continuity of safe care.

Moving forward, global nurses are expected to possess cross-cultural competence, digital literacy, and strong cross-sector collaboration. The utilization of technologies such as robotics and smart sensors must be positioned as a support for efficiency and safety, without ever removing the essence of interpersonal relationships (*human-centered care*) which remains the core of nursing service.
